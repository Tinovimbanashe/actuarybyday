Why This Matters to Me

As someone from Zimbabwe who now lives in the United States as a minority, I have experienced how global economic systems can unintentionally exclude the people they aim to serve. This perspective shapes how I think about AI, power, and responsibility in global decision-making.

Example: AI Personal Value Statement

Below is an example of a human‑centric AI personal value statement that applies the principles above to global economic leadership and policy.

Guiding Perspective

Artificial intelligence and machine learning systems are not neutral or purely technical tools. They operate within economic, political, and historical contexts shaped by colonial legacies, structural inequalities, and persistent global power asymmetries. When applied to global economic governance, AI systems risk amplifying these biases if their data, objectives, and performance metrics reflect the priorities of dominant world powers rather than the lived realities of less economically developed nations.

Core Value

Promote equity, cultural respect, and ethical accountability in the deployment of AI and machine learning within global economic systems so that innovation supports empowerment and inclusion rather than dependency or exclusion.

Ethical Lens

AI‑enabled economic development should be context‑aware and historically informed, recognising how past power structures influence present‑day data, models, and outcomes.

Strategies to Fulfil This Value

1. Acknowledge Structural Bias

AI systems should be evaluated through a historical and structural lens that accounts for colonial economic frameworks, extractive development practices, and long‑standing global power imbalances embedded in data and model design.

2. Design for Local Context

Rather than deploying standardised global models, AI systems must reflect local economic conditions, governance structures, and cultural norms through meaningful engagement with regional stakeholders.

3. Balance Decision‑Making Power

Governance frameworks should ensure that less economically developed nations have real participation in defining objectives, evaluation metrics, and policy recommendations generated by AI‑driven systems.

4. Challenge Self‑Interest‑Driven Deployment

Transparency is required to identify whose interests are embedded in AI systems used for trade, lending, risk assessment, and development planning, with accountability extending beyond efficiency to fairness and long‑term impact.

5. Foster Ethical Leadership

Ethical AI leadership requires continuous reflection and institutional learning, combining technical expertise with lived experience and local knowledge from affected communities.

6. Redefine Success

Success in AI‑driven economic policy must be measured not only by growth and predictive accuracy, but by human well‑being, sustainability, resilience, and distributive justice.

Responsible AI integration acknowledges historical bias, addresses technological power asymmetries, and prioritizes dignity, autonomy, and shared prosperity in global economic decision‑making.

Redefining Success

Growth, optimisation, and predictive accuracy are incomplete measures of progress. Ethical AI leadership prioritises long-term human well-being, trust, and shared prosperity over short-term efficiency gains.
